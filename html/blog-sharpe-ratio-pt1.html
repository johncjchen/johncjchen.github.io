Trading Metrics: On the Sharpe Ratio (Pt. 1)

November 18, 2025 Finance & Trading • 4 min read

What really is the best metric for algorithmic trading performance?

Many (including industry) would say the Sharpe ratio is the best measure of a strategy's risk-adjusted performance. The metric, in essence, compares the strategy's relative performance to a risk-free rate while accounting for the variability in those returns. This variability is defined as the standard deviation of the strategy's excess returns.

I won't go into too much of the details, much of which can just be read up on in the Investopedia article, but it should be noted that the variables for total return and return variability are not defined over the whole period but rather the incremental segments that compose the period. Please read the Investopedia article if you are still confused.

Basically, the ratio best defines risk-adjusted returns for the series of incremental time intervals which compose the backtest duration. A bigger number is better!

Limitations of the Sharpe Ratio

One issue is the assumption that a strategy's risk/volatility follows a normal distribution as calculated by the standard deviation of return variability, the ratio's denominator. Tail risk, or black swan events, are not fully represented by the ratio.

The potential illusion of a strategy with a high Sharpe ratio is aptly described by Investopedia as "picking up nickels in front of a steamroller". Consistently picking up those nickels will raise the ratio value, but one sporadic move by the steamroller can blow the strategy out of the water while barely influencing the ratio due its rarity.

Another concern is the length of the return measurement intervals, wherein longer intervals lowers volatility estimates. Monthly intervals are the standard and should be used whenever available. Otherwise, it is important to note the interval used for the Sharpe ratio calculation and to not take the ratio at face value.

Alternative Metrics

The Sharpe ratio is amazing at what it does, but I personally am slightly averse to its bidirectional focus on volatility. I'll take any upside I can! There actually is a variation of the Sharpe ratio I slightly prefer more called the Sortino ratio which only accounts for the downside risk. To me, downside deviation is the most important measure of risk for strategies I develop for algorithmic trading.

Another metric for risk-adjusted returns is the Calmar ratio, which simply compares the average annual returns to the maximum drawdown (usually over a three year period). This is only a limited view of risk due to the lack of consideration for volatility, but the Calmar ratio can be a simple metric for those looking to just evaluate maximum downside risk.
